What the job actually looks like

Way less lonely than the movies make it.

A real engineer's week is maybe a third writing new code. The rest is figuring out what to build with designers and product people, reading teammates' code before it ships, and hunting down why something broke. One survey of a thousand developers found about 42% of the week goes to fixing and improving code that already exists. Detective work, in a team.

How to get there

Four roads. I took the first one.

Computer science degree

Four years. This is my path, plus a master's after.

The most common route and the one most big employers still expect. Utah tuition, per year, before scholarships: UVU about $6,500, BYU about $7,000 for members, Utah State about $8,600, University of Utah about $9,600.

Start at a two-year school

Two years to an associate degree, then transfer or start working.

UVU and Salt Lake Community College both offer this. Same classes, lower price, and many people finish the bachelor's later while working.

Coding bootcamp

About 14 weeks, about $13,600 on average.

Fast and focused on job skills. Graduates in a 2025 survey averaged about $71,000 in their first job, with 79% employed within six months. Works best for people who already know they love this.

Self-taught with a portfolio

Free. No fixed timeline.

Build real things people can click on and put them on GitHub. Harder to get the first interview without a degree, and completely possible. Nearly half of professional developers don't hold a bachelor's degree.

Things you can do right now, at 11 or at 17, that actually matter

  1. Take the computer science class your school offers. In middle school that's often a coding elective or Scratch; in high school, AP Computer Science Principles counts.
  2. Build one thing you'd actually use: a game, a tracker for your team, a site for a family business. Finish it.
  3. Learn to use AI tools to build faster, and learn enough to know when they're wrong.
  4. In college, get an internship the summer after your first or second year. Mine led to my first job.

Think like an engineer

Find the bug.

Half the job is this. Each snippet has one mistake. Guess, then tap to check.

let total = 0;
for (let i = 1; i <= 10; i++) {
  total + i;
}
console.log(total);   // prints 0. Why?
total + i adds the numbers and then throws the result away. It needs total += i, which adds and saves. Every engineer alive has made this one.
let age = 15;
if (age = 18) {
  console.log("You can vote!");
}                      // says you can vote. You can't.
One equals sign sets age to 18. Comparing takes two: age === 18. The computer did exactly what it was told, which is the whole problem with computers.
